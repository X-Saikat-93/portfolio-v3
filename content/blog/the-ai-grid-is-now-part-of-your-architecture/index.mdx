For the last two years, AI infrastructure conversations have started with a familiar question: how many accelerators can we get into the cluster?

That question is becoming incomplete. The harder question is how many accelerators can be powered, cooled, connected, and paid for at the exact moment a workload needs them. The answer lives partly in the model-serving stack and partly in a substation, a transmission queue, a utility tariff, or a local planning decision.

This is not a metaphor. A large AI facility is a new kind of load: capital-intensive, geographically concentrated, sensitive to latency, and capable of changing its demand curve through software. Its infrastructure is now coupled to an external system with its own congestion, failure modes, and economics.

The [U.S. House vote on data-center power costs](https://apnews.com/article/ai-data-centers-congress-f073380caa61b720fa424590b5bc7c87) is one visible sign of that coupling. The bill passed 417–3 and asks state utility regulators to consider making data centers pay the full cost of new power and transmission upgrades. The federal government does not set those rates directly, but the political direction is clear: compute demand is becoming an infrastructure policy question.

The architecture consequence is simple: the grid is no longer someone else's layer.

## The load shape is part of the workload

Training and inference are both compute-heavy, but they do not create the same operational shape.

Training tends to create long, planned plateaus. A run may be expensive and inflexible, but the scheduler knows a great deal about its expected duration and resource footprint. Inference is more irregular. A product can produce a morning burst, a launch-day spike, a queue of long-context requests, or a sudden change in demand after a model release. Agentic workloads add another source of variance: a single user request can turn into a variable number of model calls, tool calls, sandbox operations, and verification passes.

The electrical system sees the aggregate of those choices. The operator sees megawatts ramping, not tokens being decoded.

That suggests a useful abstraction:

~~~text
request -> model route -> token budget -> accelerator allocation
        -> cooling envelope -> power envelope -> grid constraint
~~~

If the final step is omitted, a model scheduler can make locally rational decisions that are globally unsafe. Maximizing accelerator utilization at a site can create a thermal or power peak that forces throttling across the entire facility. Conversely, holding a cluster at a conservative fixed level can waste capacity when the grid is uncongested and renewable supply is abundant.

PUE still matters, but it is not the whole story. A facility's effective constraint includes:

- the site's contracted capacity;
- transmission and distribution limits;
- cooling headroom at the current ambient temperature;
- the ramp rate the electrical system can tolerate;
- the cost and carbon intensity of the next unit of energy;
- the portion of capacity reserved for failover;
- the operational flexibility of the workload.

Those variables belong in the same conversation as batch size, KV-cache pressure, and GPU memory.

## The power-aware scheduler

A power-aware AI scheduler does not need to know every detail of the regional grid. It does need an explicit contract with the infrastructure layer.

At minimum, each workload class should publish a power profile:

1. **Latency-critical inference.** Small bursts, strict tail-latency objectives, limited ability to move across time or region.
2. **Elastic inference.** Search, summarization, background agents, and other workloads that can accept queueing or a lower-cost model route.
3. **Training and evaluation.** Large, predictable jobs that can be scheduled against a capacity window.
4. **Speculative or opportunistic work.** Cache warming, distillation, embedding refreshes, and experiments that can stop safely.

The scheduler can then expose quality and timing modes instead of pretending every request has the same value:

~~~text
if grid_headroom is low:
    route elastic requests to smaller models
    reduce speculative decoding width
    delay non-urgent embedding refreshes
    preserve capacity for interactive traffic
elif energy_price is low and thermal_headroom is high:
    expand batch windows
    run evaluation and cache-building work
    use the available capacity before it expires
~~~

This is not permission to degrade every user experience whenever power becomes expensive. It is a way to make the tradeoff observable and deliberate. A product may offer a fast mode, a balanced mode, and a background mode. The difference is not only model selection; it can include retrieval depth, tool parallelism, verification, and the time allowed for a response.

The key metric is not accelerator utilization in isolation. It is useful work per constrained unit of capacity: completed verified tasks per kilowatt-hour, per dollar of demand charge, or per unit of reserved megawatts.

## Flexible load is a systems feature

The most interesting architectural change is that some AI workloads can become flexible loads without becoming useless.

An evaluation sweep can run at a different hour. A retrieval index can refresh in a different region. A batch of candidate responses can be sampled with fewer branches. A background coding agent can pause at a checkpoint instead of holding a worker open. A model router can select a lower-cost model when the response is not on a critical path.

The [flexible-power coalition reported by Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/16/tech-giants-launch-flexible-power-coalition-data-centers) brings technology companies, utilities, and infrastructure organizations into the same discussion. The important point is not the membership list. It is the recognition that demand flexibility is an engineering capability that can reduce both grid stress and political backlash.

This is where durable agent state becomes valuable. If an agent task can persist its plan, artifacts, and next action, the runtime can pause it cleanly. If the only state is an in-memory process and a long request timeout, the system has no safe way to move work across a power event.

The pattern looks more like a distributed job system than a chat endpoint:

~~~text
plan -> checkpoint -> reserve capacity -> execute step -> verify
                    |                         |
                    +---- reschedule ----------+
~~~

Every checkpoint has a cost. Persist too often and the system creates storage and coordination overhead. Persist too rarely and a power or host event discards expensive progress. The right interval depends on the cost of the next step and the probability of interruption.

## The hidden risk: phantom demand

There is another reason senior engineers should care about the market side of the story. Not every announced data center is a powered, financed, or customer-backed data center.

Reuters' review of the Texas pause on new data-center power requests describes utilities facing requests that, in some regions, add up to hundreds of gigawatts, while many proposals have not secured customers or actual power. That gap is sometimes called phantom demand. It can distort transmission planning, land prices, equipment orders, and investor expectations before a single model serves a request.

For a platform team, the equivalent mistake is treating a forecast as a reservation. A capacity plan that assumes every future inference request arrives at peak size will overbuy. A plan that assumes announced capacity will materialize will understate dependency risk.

A better model carries confidence levels:

- contracted capacity;
- permitted capacity;
- construction-backed capacity;
- scheduled capacity;
- forecast capacity;
- speculative capacity.

The same accounting should apply to software capacity. A model route that exists in a benchmark is not production capacity. A provider region listed on a roadmap is not a failover region. A reserved quota with no tested admission path is not resilience.

## What to instrument now

The practical starting point is a joined view of application and facility telemetry. For each request or job, capture:

- model, quantization, and accelerator type;
- input and output tokens;
- prefill and decode time;
- tool calls and sandbox duration;
- batch position and queue delay;
- watts or estimated energy for the execution window;
- location and power-price or carbon-intensity signal;
- whether the job was interruptible;
- quality or verification outcome.

The last field matters. Saving energy by returning an unverified answer is not an optimization; it is cost transfer to the user. The target should be energy-aware quality, not energy-blind throughput.

Over time, this telemetry enables better placement decisions. A long-context retrieval job may be cheaper at a different region. An interactive agent may be worth keeping close to the user even when a background evaluation job moves. A high-value verification pass may justify its energy cost because it prevents an expensive downstream error.

## The architecture decision

Power does not replace the usual concerns of distributed AI systems. It adds another boundary where the system can be slow, expensive, constrained, or unavailable.

The durable design is therefore familiar:

1. Make the constraint visible in the scheduler.
2. Classify work by urgency and interruptibility.
3. Checkpoint state before expensive steps.
4. Give the product explicit quality and timing modes.
5. Measure useful, verified work instead of raw utilization.
6. Keep capacity forecasts separate from capacity commitments.

The next generation of AI platforms will still compete on model quality and serving latency. They will also compete on whether they can turn a volatile, expensive load into a controllable one. The grid is becoming part of the architecture because the architecture is now large enough to affect the grid.
